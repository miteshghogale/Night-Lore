[intrigued] In the pitch-black night inside Amidaji Temple near the Shimonoseki Straits, a blind young minstrel named Hoichi sits cross-legged on the tatami mats, plucking the four heavy gut strings of his biwa with a horn plectrum. His voice swells with breathtaking emotion, chanting the tragic saga of the Heike clan. In the darkness around him, unseen listeners weep quietly, the sound of iron armor clanking softly in the shadows whenever he recounts a fatal sword strike or the crashing of warships. Hoichi cannot see the stern samurai standing beside him, nor the noble lady in ancient court robes seated before him. He believes he is performing inside the magnificent hall of a wealthy nobleman's palace. In reality, he is sitting alone in the middle of a deserted cemetery, surrounded by glowing spectral lanterns, mossy tombstones, and the restless dead.

[somber] The tale of Hoichi the Earless is one of Japan's most celebrated literary ghost stories. Hoichi lived at Amidaji Temple in the port city of Shimonoseki, possessing extraordinary talent with the biwa, a traditional four-stringed Japanese lute. Blind from early childhood, he had dedicated his life to memorizing and reciting The Tale of the Heike, an epic poem recounting the tragic downfall of the Taira clan during the catastrophic civil war of the late twelfth century. Hoichi's renditions were so emotionally intense that even hardened warriors shed tears when he sang of the final naval battle at Dan-no-ura, portraying the desperate shouts of dying captains and the heavy splashing of armor-laden bodies into the sea.

[tense] One midnight, while the temple's head priest was away attending to a dying parishioner, a towering retainer armored in dark steel appeared at the temple gates. The intruder commanded Hoichi to come with him to perform for his lord, a high-born noble residing nearby with his entire court. Hoichi agreed, picking up his biwa and following the heavy, metallic footsteps of the retainer through the storm.

[grave] For six consecutive nights, the retainer led Hoichi away into the dark. Each night, Hoichi sang the battle chapters with intense passion, marveling at the emotional response of his aristocratic audience. However, the nocturnal journeys began to drain his physical vitality. He returned to the temple before dawn pale, exhausted, and feverish. Suspicious of Hoichi's secretive midnight disappearances, the head priest ordered two temple servants to track the musician into the dark.

[urgent] The servants followed Hoichi into the driving rain, tracking the sound of his biwa to the Amidaji cemetery. There, they found the blind musician playing frantically in front of the tomb of the drowned child emperor Antoku. Skeletal hands applauded from the darkness as blue spectral flames, known as oni-bi, hovered over the surrounding graves. Recognizing that Hoichi was under the thrall of vengeful Heike ghosts who would consume his life force, the servants dragged the trembling musician back to the temple.

[measured] To shield Hoichi from the spirits, the head priest devised a sacred ritual. Using black sumi ink, the priest brush-painted the holy characters of the Heart Sutra across every inch of Hoichi's body, writing the sacred text over his face, eyelids, chest, arms, legs, and back, rendering him spiritually invisible to ghosts. However, in his haste before dusk, the priest forgot to paint the sutra characters onto Hoichi's ears.

[tense] That night, when the spectral warrior arrived at the temple, he searched the courtyard and temple halls but saw no trace of the musician. Looking closely into the dark air, the warrior spotted two detached ears floating mid-air, the only parts of Hoichi left un-protected by the holy characters. Realizing he could not return to his ghostly lord empty-handed, the samurai tore off Hoichi's ears as proof of his attempt and carried them back into the night. Though severely mutilated, Hoichi survived, freed forever from the spirit court.

[calm] How did a medieval ghost story about a blind lute player evolve into a world-famous masterpiece of horror cinema? The journey of Hoichi the Earless reflects a seamless throughline connecting tragic history, Buddhist ritual practice, classic literature, and modern film.

[curious] The legend is anchored in the Battle of Dan-no-ura, fought on April 25, 1185, in the narrow Shimonoseki Straits. In this catastrophic naval battle, the Minamoto clan wiped out the rival Taira clan. Seeing defeat, grandmother Taira no Tokiko jumped into the sea carrying six-year-old Emperor Antoku and the sacred imperial sword, drowning along with hundreds of samurai. Distressed by reports of ghostly lights and crying on the waters, local residents built Amidaji Temple (now Akama Shrine) to appease the drowned spirits. Fishermen to this day point to Heike-gani, small local crabs whose shells resemble scowling samurai faces.

[measured] Hoichi himself represents the biwa hoshi, blind traveling minstrels of medieval Japan. Reciting epic poetry accompanied by the biwa was not merely performance; in Buddhist tradition, these recitations served a sacred function called chinkon, the ritual pacification of angry or un-quiet spirits. By chanting the tragic courage of the Heike, biwa hoshi helped soothe the restless souls of fallen warriors, preventing them from turning into destructive onryo.

[intrigued] This cultural tradition was first captured in print in 1782, when author Isseki Sanjin published the story in his collection Gayu Kidan. Over a century later, Greek-Irish writer Lafcadio Hearn (who adopted the Japanese name Koizumi Yakumo) translated and refined the tale in his landmark 1904 book Kwaidan, introducing Hoichi to an international audience.

[reflective] That literary legacy reached its artistic peak in 1964, when director Masaki Kobayashi adapted Hearn's story into his iconic anthology film Kwaidan. Starring Katsuo Nakamura as Hoichi and Takashi Shimura as the priest, Kobayashi's film combined hand-painted sets, theatrical lighting, and Toru Takemitsu's avant-garde biwa score. Winning the Special Jury Prize at the 1965 Cannes Film Festival and earning an Academy Award nomination, Kobayashi's film completed Hoichi's journey from a 12th-century naval catastrophe to the heights of world cinema.
