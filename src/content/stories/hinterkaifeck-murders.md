## Grounded in Winter Mud

In late March 1922, the Bavarian landscape surrounding the hamlet of Gröbern remained locked in a damp, lingering winter cold. Located approximately seventy kilometers north of Munich, between the regional hubs of Ingolstadt and Schrobenhausen, the area was dominated by flat agricultural clearings bordered by the dense pine woods of the Waidhofen forest. Set roughly several hundred meters apart from the main village stood Hinterkaifeck—a secluded, solitary farmstead named simply for its geographic position: "behind Kaifeck."

Hinterkaifeck was a self-contained homestead comprising a residence, maid quarters, livestock barn, and grain store, all housed under adjoining roofs. It was inhabited by six people: patriarch Andreas Gruber, aged sixty-three; his wife Cäzilia Gruber, aged seventy-two; their widowed daughter Viktoria Gabriel, aged thirty-five; Viktoria’s seven-year-old daughter Cäzilia Gabriel; Viktoria’s two-year-old son Josef; and a newly hired maid, forty-four-year-old Maria Baumgartner.

To outward observers, the routine at Hinterkaifeck was defined by heavy physical labor and social isolation. The family maintained a modest herd of cattle, kept swine, and cultivated the surrounding fields. However, regional rumors and village gossip frequently isolated the family from their neighbors in Gröbern and Kaifeck. Court records from previous years documented bitter legal disputes over property boundaries, as well as incest allegations surrounding Andreas Gruber and his daughter Viktoria. Following the death of Viktoria’s husband, Karl Gabriel, who was reported killed in the trenches of France in 1914 during the First World War, the household turned further inward, relying primarily on their own labor to maintain the farm.

On the afternoon of Friday, March 31, 1922, forty-four-year-old Maria Baumgartner arrived at Hinterkaifeck to begin her employment as the household maid. Accompanied by her sister, who walked with her to the property boundary before returning home, Maria carried her personal belongings into the farmstead. She had been hired to replace the family's previous maid, who had abruptly left the farm six months earlier. As darkness fell over the Bavarian countryside that Friday evening, the six inhabitants locked the heavy oak doors of Hinterkaifeck against the night wind. None of them would ever be seen alive again.

## The Tracks into the Snow

The horror that descended upon Hinterkaifeck on the night of March 31 was preceded by weeks of documented, unexplained anomalies—events that the family had shared with neighbors, local tradesmen, and visitors in the days leading up to their deaths.

Roughly a week before the killings, a late-winter snowfall covered the fields and pine woods surrounding Gröbern. Walking near the edge of the property, Andreas Gruber discovered a distinct trail of human footprints originating from the tree line of the Waidhofen forest. The tracks cut across the fresh snow, leading straight across the open field directly toward the rear door of the farmstead's engine house. Intrigued and alarmed, Gruber traced the footsteps carefully around the perimeter of the building. He searched for a corresponding set of prints leading back toward the woods or out toward the main road. There were none. Whoever had walked out of the dark forest and approached the house had never left.

In the days following the discovery of the footprints, the atmosphere inside the house grew increasingly tense. Andreas Gruber reported to neighbors that a set of house keys—essential for locking the main doors and exterior gates—had vanished from the kitchen without explanation. No member of the family could locate them. 

Shortly thereafter, Gruber discovered an unfamiliar object inside the house: a printed Munich newspaper sitting on a table in the living quarters. Gruber questioned his family and checked with local postmen and neighbors; no subscriber lived at Hinterkaifeck, no one in the household had purchased a paper, and no news vendor serviced that isolated road. The newspaper had been brought into the house by hand.

Nightfall brought further disturbances. Both Andreas Gruber and Viktoria Gabriel mentioned hearing heavy, deliberate footsteps echoing from the attic space directly above their bedchambers late at night. Gruber took a lantern and a heavy stick to inspect the attic, climbing the narrow wooden stairs and sifting through stored grain and loose straw. He found no intruder, yet floorboards continued to creak in the dark hours after the household went to sleep. Additionally, family members noted that the lock on the door to the farm's machine room had been broken.

These strange occurrences echoed the warnings of the family's previous maid, who had resigned six months earlier in autumn 1921. Upon leaving Hinterkaifeck, she had told relatives and acquaintances in the surrounding villages that she refused to stay on the property any longer, insisting that the farmstead was haunted by an unseen presence and that she constantly heard footsteps overhead in the timbers.

## March 31, 1922 — The Silence in the Barn

On the evening of Friday, March 31, 1922, the sequence of events turned from quiet apprehension into methodical violence. Based on the crime scene layout and autopsy findings, investigators reconstructed that the killer or killers positioned themselves inside the structure after dark.

The attack began in the barn. During the evening hours, individual members of the family were drawn one by one out of the living quarters and into the unlit barn space. It is believed that unusual noises among the cattle or a deliberate disturbance created by the intruder lured the victims into the darkness of the outbuilding.

Andreas Gruber was the first to enter. As he stepped through the heavy wooden door separating the residence from the stable area, he was struck down with catastrophic force by an attacker waiting in the shadows. Shortly thereafter, his wife, seventy-two-year-old Cäzilia Gruber, followed him into the barn, where she was immediately attacked. Thirty-five-year-old Viktoria Gabriel was lured next into the structure, followed by her seven-year-old daughter, young Cäzilia. 

Each of the four victims was struck repeatedly about the head and face with a sharp, heavy farm implement. The attacker utilized a mattock—a heavy, hoe-like digging tool featuring a flat cutting blade on one side and a pick point on the other, which belonged to the farmstead. The strikes crushed skull bones and caused immediate incapacitation. Once all four were dead or unconscious, the killer stacked their bodies on top of one another on the earthen floor near the door, covering the pile with loose straw and a discarded wooden door panel that had been unhinged from a stall.

With the four victims in the barn secured beneath the straw, the killer moved from the outbuilding into the main residence. 

Walking down the quiet hallway, the attacker entered the maid's bedchamber. Maria Baumgartner, who had arrived at Hinterkaifeck only hours earlier, was killed in her bed before she had even finished unpacking her travel bags. She suffered fatal blows to the head from the same mattock while lying on her mattress. 

Finally, the killer entered the living quarters. Resting inside his wooden cot was two-year-old Josef. The attacker struck the sleeping toddler in the head with the weapon, killing him instantly inside his crib.

## The Four Days of Smoke

The slaughter of the six inhabitants of Hinterkaifeck was complete before midnight on March 31, 1922. However, the tragedy did not end with the final blow. In one of the most chilling aspects of the case, the perpetrator did not flee the scene into the surrounding Waidhofen forest. Instead, the killer remained on the farmstead, occupying the house alongside the six corpses for four full days.

From Saturday, April 1, through Tuesday, April 4, normal life appeared to continue at Hinterkaifeck when viewed from the outside. Neighbors and local farmers traveling along the road between Gröbern and Kaifeck observed smoke rising regularly from the farmhouse chimney during morning and evening mealtimes. The killer utilized the kitchen stove, kindling fires, baking bread, and preparing meals from the family's stored provisions. Food items in the pantry—including bread, meat, and lard—were consumed in significant quantities over those four days.

Inside the barn and stables, the farm's livestock required constant maintenance. The killer tended to the animals throughout the four-day period. The cattle were fed, provided with fresh water, and milked on schedule. The family dog, a spitz, was tied up near the barn entrance; though it had suffered a minor blow to its eye, it was fed and watered by the occupant. 

On Saturday, April 1, two coffee merchants named Hans Schirovsky and Eduard Schirovsky arrived at Hinterkaifeck to take an order. Finding no one answering the front door, they walked around the courtyard, knocked on the windows, and noted that the farm was entirely quiet except for the sound of cattle inside the barn and the barking of the dog. Receiving no response, they left.

On Monday, April 3, a local craftsman named Michael Plöckl passed by Hinterkaifeck and noticed that the oven in the farm's bakehouse was smoking. As he walked near the building, he saw a person standing near the bakehouse; when Plöckl approached, the figure turned away and walked out of sight. Assuming it was Andreas Gruber tending his oven, Plöckl continued on his way.

Because smoke continued to rise from the chimney and the livestock remained quiet, the surrounding community assumed the Gruber family was simply occupied with indoor farm work or resting over the weekend. The presence of the killer inside the home created a seamless illusion of routine, masking the horror contained within the barn and bedrooms.

## April 4 Discovery & Autopsy Evidence

By Tuesday, April 4, 1922, subtle irregularities began to arouse suspicion among the residents of neighboring Gröbern.

Seven-year-old Cäzilia Gabriel had been absent from her primary school classes on both Saturday, April 1, and Monday, April 3, without notice from her mother. Furthermore, the local postman noted that mail delivered to Hinterkaifeck had begun piling up untouched in the farmstead’s postbox. 

Concerned by these uncharacteristic disruptions, neighbor Lorenz Schlittenbauer decided to investigate. Schlittenbauer, a farmer whose land bordered Hinterkaifeck, recruited two fellow villagers—Jakob Sigl and Michael Pöll—to accompany him to the Gruber property on the afternoon of April 4.

Arriving at the farmstead shortly after three o'clock in the afternoon, the three men found the gates locked and the house silent. Calling out for Andreas Gruber yielded no reply. Schlittenbauer led Sigl and Pöll toward the barn door, which was unlocked. Pushing open the heavy wooden door, the men stepped into the dim interior.

Near the doorway, Schlittenbauer noticed a pile of straw and a displaced wooden door panel lying awkwardly on the dirt floor. Pulling back the straw, Schlittenbauer uncovered the bloodied foot of a human body. Moving the wooden panel aside, the men discovered the stacked remains of Andreas Gruber, Cäzilia Gruber, Viktoria Gabriel, and seven-year-old Cäzilia Gabriel. 

Leaving the barn in shock, Schlittenbauer, Sigl, and Pöll entered the main residence through the adjoining doorway. In the maid's bedroom, they found the body of Maria Baumgartner lying across her bed. Moving further into the living quarters, they discovered two-year-old Josef dead inside his wooden cot.

Schlittenbauer immediately dispatched word to local municipal authorities in Schrobenhausen, who alerted the Royal Bavarian State Police in Munich. Later that evening, Inspector Georg Reingruber arrived at Hinterkaifeck from Munich alongside a team of forensic investigators, criminalists, and police dog handlers.

On Wednesday, April 5, court pathologist Dr. Johann Baptist Aumüller conducted formal post-mortem examinations on site inside the farmstead. Dr. Aumüller’s autopsy reports documented that all six victims had died from severe blunt force trauma to the head, resulting in massive skull fractures and intracranial hemorrhaging. The weapon was confirmed to be the farm’s own mattock, which was recovered on site bearing dried blood and hair.

Dr. Aumüller’s physical examination yielded a horrifying detail regarding seven-year-old Cäzilia Gabriel. While five of the victims had died almost instantly from their injuries, the autopsy revealed that young Cäzilia had survived the initial blow for several hours. As she lay trapped beneath the bodies of her mother and grandparents in the dark barn, the child had pulled out small tufts of her own hair in agony before dying of shock and blood loss.

In searching the living quarters, Inspector Reingruber’s team conducted a thorough audit of the family’s assets. Investigators found large sums of paper Reichsmarks, gold coins, and personal jewelry left completely untouched in unlocked drawers and cupboards throughout the house. The killer had lived in the house for four days without taking the family's accumulated wealth, effectively disproving simple robbery as the primary motive for the crime.

## What Actually Happened Here

More than a century after Inspector Georg Reingruber stepped onto the bloodstained floorboards of Hinterkaifeck, the murders remain one of Europe's most famous unsolved criminal cases. Over the decades, Bavarian police interviewed numerous suspects, took extensive witness testimony, and submitted the case to recurring reviews, yet no individual was ever successfully charged.

Several primary theories emerged from the police files:

### The Lorenz Schlittenbauer Theory
Lorenz Schlittenbauer remained a central person of interest for decades. Schlittenbauer had engaged in a romantic relationship with Viktoria Gabriel prior to the murders and had previously been named in court as the alleged father of two-year-old Josef, though he later disputed paternity. Villagers noted that Schlittenbauer’s reaction upon discovering the bodies was unusually calm; he walked directly through the dark barn without hesitation, moved the bodies before police arrived, and unlocked house doors with apparent familiarity. However, Schlittenbauer consistently maintained his innocence, successfully won several civil libel lawsuits against neighbors who publicly accused him, and died in 1941 without any physical evidence linking him to the murder weapon.

### The Gump Brothers & Paramilitary Connection
Another major avenue of inquiry centered on Adolf and Anton Gump. Adolf Gump was initially suspected due to his affiliation with the Freikorps Oberland—a right-wing paramilitary organization—and his alleged involvement in violent incidents in Upper Silesia. Interest in the brothers resurfaced in 1951 when their sister, Kreszentia Mayer, made a deathbed statement to a priest accusing her brothers Adolf and Anton of committing the Hinterkaifeck murders. Bavarian police reopened the investigation and interrogated Anton Gump (Adolf had died in 1944), but no corroborating evidence could be established, and charges were never filed.

### The Unidentified Stalker
The physical evidence compiled by Inspector Reingruber—the single set of footprints leading from the Waidhofen woods, the missing keys, the Munich newspaper, the broken lock on the machine room door, and the footsteps overhead—points strongly toward an unknown intruder who stalked the household from the attic space prior to March 31. This theory suggests an individual of extreme psychological detachment who observed the family’s daily schedule from above, executed the slaughter with cold efficiency, and then occupied the domestic space of his victims for four days before slipping back into the Bavarian woods.

In 2007, students at the Police Academy of Fürstenfeldbruck utilized modern criminalistic methods and advanced forensic technology to analyze the historical case files. While the academy concluded that modern forensic techniques could pinpoint a primary suspect, their final report was kept permanently confidential out of respect for living descendants of the named individuals.

Today, the physical structure of Hinterkaifeck no longer exists; the farmstead was completely demolished in 1923, and its timber and stone were cleared away. In its place stands a simple stone monument surrounded by open Bavarian fields—a quiet marker for six victims whose final nights remain shrouded in unresolved horror.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Were the Hinterkaifeck murders ever officially solved?
No, the murders were never officially solved and no suspect was ever formally charged. Although Munich police interviewed numerous suspects over the decades and a 2007 police academy review identified a likely perpetrator using modern techniques, the report's conclusions remain permanently confidential out of respect for living descendants.

### Is the Hinterkaifeck farmstead still standing today?
No, the entire Hinterkaifeck farmstead was completely demolished in 1923, one year after the killings. Today, no physical structures remain, and the isolated site near Gröbern is marked by a simple stone memorial.

### Why was robbery ruled out as the primary motive for the crime?
Robbery was ruled out because large sums of paper currency, gold coins, and personal jewelry were found untouched in unlocked drawers throughout the house. Furthermore, the perpetrator occupied the home for four days following the murders without taking any of the family's accumulated wealth.

### What happened to Lorenz Schlittenbauer, the prime suspect?
Lorenz Schlittenbauer was heavily investigated due to his past relationship with Viktoria Gabriel, a paternity dispute over toddler Josef, and his surprisingly calm behavior when discovering the bodies. However, he maintained his innocence throughout his life, successfully won civil libel lawsuits against neighbors who publicly accused him, and died in 1941 without any physical evidence linking him to the crime.

### Did the killer really live on the farm with the bodies after the murders?
Yes, forensic evidence and witness sightings established that the perpetrator occupied the farmstead for four days from March 31 to April 4, 1922. During this time, the killer kindled fires in the stove, cooked meals from the family's provisions, and routinely fed and milked the farm's livestock.
