> **STATUS: UNRESOLVED**  
> *Justification:* Despite dozens of official investigations by U.S. intelligence agencies, international scientific panels, and medical research centers since 2016, no single cause or perpetrator has been established, and evidence remains genuinely split between directed-energy and psychogenic hypotheses.

## The Auditory Anomaly in Havana: Origins of a Modern Enigma

In late 2016, inside quiet residential neighborhoods and hotel rooms across Havana, Cuba, a series of disturbing medical events began among foreign diplomats. American and Canadian government officials, including CIA officers, State Department personnel, and family members, reported the sudden onset of unexplained neurological symptoms. Many described the experience as starting with a perceived localized, directional loud sound, characterized as sharp screeching, high-pitched chirping, metallic clicking, or piercing grating noises.

The physical sensations accompanying these acoustic occurrences were distinct. Diplomats reported intense localized pressure, heat, or vibration in their heads, comparing the feeling to driving a vehicle with a window partially rolled down. These phenomena lasted from twenty seconds to thirty minutes, occurring while personnel were at home or staying in hotel rooms. Mysteriously, family members or guests in adjacent rooms heard nothing and experienced no symptoms, highlighting the localized nature of the incidents.

The acute neurological aftermath was immediate. Affected individuals suffered from severe dizziness, ear pain, diffuse head pain, nausea, tinnitus, and hearing loss. Two-thirds reported visual disturbances, including blurred vision and light sensitivity. Over half experienced head pressure, vibration, and cognitive issues such as forgetfulness and poor concentration. One-quarter suffered dizziness or unsteady gait. For many, acute symptoms evolved into chronic conditions lasting months, marked by balance disorders, insomnia, and cognitive fatigue.

As reports reached twenty-six affected Americans in Cuba by June 2018, diplomatic tensions escalated. In August 2017, the U.S. State Department concluded the health problems resulted from an attack or unknown device, though it did not explicitly blame Cuba. In response, the U.S. expelled two Cuban diplomats in August 2017, removed non-essential embassy staff in September 2017, and issued a travel advisory. In October 2017, President Donald Trump called the occurrences a very unusual attack and blamed Cuba. In March 2018, the State Department extended staff reductions indefinitely under ordered departure status. Global Affairs Canada similarly ended family postings to Havana in early 2018 and reduced embassy staff in 2019 after a fourteenth Canadian diplomat reported symptoms in late December 2018. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police struggled to investigate due to the unknown cause.

## The Global Contagion: From Guangzhou to the Ellipse

What began in Havana quickly expanded into a global phenomenon. Beginning in late 2017 and accelerating through 2018 and 2021, suspected anomalous health incidents (AHIs) were reported by U.S. intelligence, diplomatic, and military personnel worldwide.

The first major outbreak outside Cuba occurred in early 2018 in China. An American diplomat at the U.S. Consulate General in Guangzhou reported persistent symptoms dating back to late 2017. In May 2018, Secretary of State Mike Pompeo testified before the House Foreign Affairs Committee that symptoms in Guangzhou were very similar to and entirely consistent with those from Cuba. By June 2018, multiple diplomats were evacuated from Guangzhou, prompting the State Department to establish a task force and expand its health warning to all of mainland China. The advisory instructed personnel experiencing unusual acute auditory or sensory phenomena accompanied by piercing noises not to attempt to locate the sound source.

Reports soon emerged from numerous international posts, including Moscow (Russia), Tbilisi (Georgia), Poland, Taiwan, and Australia, where the Russian Embassy publicly dismissed reports of Russian operatives targeting CIA personnel. Reports also came from Tashkent (Uzbekistan), where a USAID employee reported an incident in September 2017 that was discounted by the State Department, as well as Kyrgyzstan, Serbia, London, and Bogota (Colombia). In London, three White House staffers reported symptoms at the InterContinental London Park Lane in May 2019. In Serbia, the CIA evacuated an intelligence officer in 2021 following a suspected attack. In August 2021, the evacuation of two American diplomats from Hanoi, Vietnam, delayed a scheduled visit by Vice President Kamala Harris. In September 2021, an aide-de-camp traveling with CIA Director William J. Burns reported AHI symptoms during a diplomatic visit to India.

Vienna, Austria, reported the largest cluster outside Havana, with dozens of U.S. personnel and children affected in 2021. The outbreak led to joint investigations with the Austrian Foreign Ministry. In September 2021, the CIA recalled its station chief in Vienna following internal criticism over slow responses to cases.

Domestic locations were also affected. In 2019, a White House official reported debilitating symptoms while walking her dog in a Virginia suburb of Washington, D.C. In November 2020, a similar incident occurred on The Ellipse near the White House. Defense Department officials briefed Senate and House Armed Services Committees in April 2021, acknowledging that investigators could not determine the cause or perpetrators. By early 2022, media estimates indicated over 1,000 individuals had reported symptoms globally, though a July 2024 Government Accountability Office (GAO) report clarified that 334 individuals completed qualification for military health system care.

## The Directed-Energy Weapon Hypothesis and NASEM Assessment

U.S. agencies and research institutions launched scientific evaluations to determine the cause of AHIs. One primary theory was the directed-energy weapon hypothesis, positing that targeted pulses of radio frequency (RF) or microwave energy were being directed at U.S. personnel.

Early speculation focused on acoustic weapons and infrasound. In March 2018, University of Michigan computer scientists suggested that intermodulation distortion from multiple inaudible ultrasonic signals from malfunctioning or improperly placed Cuban surveillance equipment could produce audible sound. However, experts doubted acoustic energy alone could cause neurological damage without causing severe eardrum injury.

Attention shifted toward pulsed microwave radiation. In December 2020, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) released a report by a standing committee associated in research citations with Dr. David Relman. The NASEM committee reviewed clinical symptoms and literature, concluding that directed, pulsed radio frequency energy was the most plausible mechanism explaining acute symptoms, particularly localized auditory sensations and head pressure.

Subsequent government reviews in 2022 further examined electromagnetic mechanisms through two separate evaluations. In February 2022, an expert scientific panel convened by the Director of National Intelligence (DNI) and the CIA assessed that pulsed RF energy and ultrasound were plausible mechanisms to explain cases presenting four core symptoms. Separately, on February 10, 2022, the State Department released unclassified portions of a report by the JASON Advisory Group. The 2022 JASON report conducted a frequency-range analysis, concluding that RF frequencies between 500 MHz and 30 GHz were a highly unlikely cause, while ruling out ionizing radiation, acoustic energy, and RF frequencies below 500 MHz or above 30 GHz. Additionally, an interim CIA report in January 2022 studying roughly 1,000 cases noted that while most incidents were explained by environmental factors, medical conditions, or stress, about two dozen unexplained cases remained where foreign actor involvement could not be ruled out.

## The Psychogenic Hypothesis, Cricket Recordings, and Methodological Critiques

Parallel to the directed-energy hypothesis, a scientific counter-explanation proposed that Havana syndrome was largely driven by mass psychogenic illness, stress reactions, and environmental misattributions. Proponents argued that occupational stress, geopolitical anxieties, and social contagion transformed physical symptoms into a perceived foreign attack.

Assessments of the environmental hypothesis involved two distinct, independent investigations into the recorded noises. First, in 2018, the State Department commissioned the JASON Advisory Group, a panel of government scientists, to evaluate sound recordings provided by diplomats in Havana. JASON concluded with high confidence that the recorded sounds were most likely insect noises rather than a technological sonic weapon, noting that no single energy source could produce both the recorded audio and reported medical effects. Separately, in January 2019, biologists Alexander L. Stubbs of the University of California, Berkeley, and Fernando Montealegre-Z of the University of Lincoln conducted an independent academic study analyzing the same recordings. By matching the pulse repetition rate, power spectrum, pulse rate stability, and oscillations per pulse, Stubbs and Montealegre-Z independently identified the noise as the calling song of the Indies short-tailed cricket (*Anurogryllus celerinictus*).

Scientific scrutiny also focused on early neuroimaging studies claiming to document brain injury. At the U.S. government's request, University of Pennsylvania (UPenn) researchers examined twenty-one diplomats posted to Cuba. Preliminary results published in JAMA in 2018 by Swanson et al., followed by a 2019 neuroimaging study by Verma et al., reported finding statistically significant differences in white matter structure between AHI patients and controls.

However, the UPenn findings faced immediate methodological criticism. A JAMA editorial highlighted major limitations, including the lack of baseline pre-exposure brain scans, small control groups, reliance on subjective self-reports, and non-specific MRI findings. Critics noted that white matter variations observed on MRIs can result from common conditions like depression, migraine, or normal variation. In late 2018, the editorial board of Cortex, led by Sergio Della Sala, published a critique citing gross methodological flaws and asking the authors to clarify methods or retract the study. Neurologists like Dr. Robert Baloh and bioengineers Kenneth R. Foster and Peter Zimmerman publicly dismissed the microwave conclusions, with Baloh describing the UPenn interpretations as science fiction.

In September 2021, the Cuban Academy of Sciences published an independent report by twenty-one scientists. The panel concluded the directed-energy weapon hypothesis was scientifically unsustainable, asserting U.S. studies cherry-picked data. The committee found no physical evidence of an electromagnetic or acoustic attack, attributing cases to mass psychogenic illness, pre-existing conditions, and environmental noise.

Further support for the psychogenic model appeared in medical literature throughout 2019 and 2023. Sociologist Robert Bartholomew and neurologist Robert Baloh argued that Havana syndrome represented a mass psychogenic illness reinforced by a nocebo effect. In 2023, articles in the International Journal of Social Psychiatry underscored that Havana syndrome was a socially constructed catch-all category, lumping pre-existing conditions, stress responses, and environmental exposures under a single label.

## The 2023 Intelligence Assessment and the 2024 NIH Replications

The definitive turning point in official evaluations occurred in March 2023, with the publication of the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) intelligence community assessment. Representing seven U.S. intelligence agencies after more than two years of investigation, the ODNI report stands as the most authoritative government conclusion to date.

The 2023 ODNI assessment concluded it was very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for causing reported anomalous health incidents. Five agencies judged foreign adversary involvement to be very unlikely, one judged it unlikely, and one abstained. Intelligence analysts determined symptoms were most likely the result of pre-existing medical conditions, conventional illnesses, and environmental factors. CIA Director William J. Burns praised the investigation, noting agencies applied rigorous operational, technical, and analytical tradecraft.

The conclusions drew sharp political reactions. Former National Security Adviser John Bolton expressed skepticism regarding Cuban involvement, revealing he had long doubted Cuba possessed the technology or political motivation to conduct such attacks. Conversely, Cuba's Vice Foreign Minister Carlos Fernandez de Cossio welcomed the findings while accusing the U.S. government of leveraging baseless attack claims to derail bilateral relations. In Congress, Senator Marco Rubio, vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, rejected the consensus, insisting unresolved cases warranted continued review.

The scientific foundation of the 2023 assessment received empirical backing in March 2024, when the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Clinical Center published two major studies in JAMA. Conducted over five years by NIH researchers including Pierpaoli et al. and Chan et al., the studies evaluated two cohorts of federal employees (eighty-one and eighty-six participants) who reported AHIs against closely matched control groups.

Using state-of-the-art MRI neuroimaging, blood biomarkers, and clinical evaluations, NIH researchers failed to replicate the earlier UPenn findings. The NIH studies revealed no significant differences in brain structure, volume, white matter integrity, or physiological function between AHI patients and control participants after adjusting for multiple comparisons. Researchers concluded there was no evidence of persistent structural brain injury or unique neurobiological markers associated with Havana syndrome, though noting many patients suffered high levels of stress and anxiety requiring medical care.

## Recent Developments (2025-2026): Unconfirmed Reporting and Evolving Theories

Developments occurring between 2025 and 2026 present recent and unconfirmed proposals rather than settled scientific findings. These recent reports should be viewed with appropriate nuance and distinguished from the established consensus built between 2017 and 2024.

In January 2025, the U.S. intelligence community issued a formal update to its 2023 assessment. The update confirmed that five of seven intelligence components continue to assess that it is very unlikely a foreign actor caused reported AHIs, noting newly acquired intelligence remained consistent with prior judgments. However, the update noted two components subtly adjusted technical assessments regarding foreign technological capabilities to produce biological effects, leading one component to maintain it remained unlikely a foreign actor actually deployed such a weapon in any reported event.

Media reporting in 2026 introduced further unconfirmed claims regarding potential hardware. Reports alleged that Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) acquired a portable, backpack-sized device emitting pulsed radio frequencies during an undercover operation toward the end of the Biden administration. According to media coverage by CBS, the device contained components of Russian origin, though intelligence officials and independent scientists expressed strong skepticism regarding its connection to AHIs, and testing remains ongoing.

Other recent hypotheses include a February 2026 report in The Washington Post describing a 2024 experiment where a Norwegian government scientist constructed a portable microwave device and tested it on himself, suffering transient symptoms that strengthened debate over microwave biological effects. Additionally, in January 2026, physicist Alexander Kostinsky proposed an optoacoustic laser hypothesis, arguing that pulsed infrared lasers could induce neurological symptoms via thermoelastic sound waves without high-power microwave hardware. In March 2026, the publication T-invariant noted that while scientists found no immediate technical flaws in the optoacoustic model, concerns regarding retinal damage remained unaddressed. Finally, 2026 marked the death of the first U.S. official who reported symptoms in Havana in 2016.

Eight years after the first grating sounds were heard in Havana, Havana syndrome remains one of the most complex puzzles in modern geopolitical history. With medical studies showing no persistent structural brain damage and intelligence agencies finding no evidence of a covert foreign campaign, the phenomenon sits at the uneasy intersection of neurology, environmental science, psychological contagion, and international politics.
