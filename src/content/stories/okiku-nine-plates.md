Deep in the quiet hours of a dark Edo night, a faint, weeping voice rises from the stone mouth of a deserted manor well. Slow and trembling, the ghost of a young servant maiden begins counting her master's heirloom porcelain plates. She counts them one by one in a hollow whisper: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. Upon reaching nine, the counting abruptly ceases. A long silence hangs over the courtyard before a piercing shriek of despair echoes into the night, followed by bitter sobbing as the spirit realizes the tenth plate remains missing.

The legend of Okiku is one of Japan's most celebrated kaidan, or ghost tales, centered on the terrifying rise of an onryo, a vengeful spirit driven by betrayal. According to traditional narrative accounts, Okiku was a young maid serving in the household of a high-ranking samurai named Aoyama Tessan. Okiku was tasked with the care of ten imported Dutch ceramic plates, precious treasures representing immense wealth and political status.

Aoyama harbored a deep obsession for Okiku and repeatedly pressured her to become his mistress. Dedicated to her duties and honor, Okiku consistently rejected his advances. Infuriated by her refusals, Aoyama devised a cruel scheme to coerce her. He stole one of the ten ceramic plates and hid it within his private quarters, then summoned Okiku to inspect the set.

When Okiku opened the storage box and discovered only nine plates, terror took hold. In Edo period Japan, losing or damaging a feudal lord's heirloom was a severe offense punishable by torture or death. Desperate, Okiku counted the remaining plates dozens of times, lining them up across the tatami mats, but always came up short. Aoyama then offered her a choice: submit to his desires, and he would overlook the missing plate, or refuse and face immediate execution for theft.

Okiku refused to give in. Enraged by her unyielding spirit, Aoyama had his guards bind her with heavy ropes, beat her severely, and suspend her over the manor well. When she still refused his demands, he struck her down and cast her body into the dark water below, sealing the well cover over her corpse.

Within days of the murder, the ghost of Okiku emerged from the depths of the well each night, tormenting Aoyama and his household with her haunting count. Driven toward madness by the relentless apparition, Aoyama saw his reputation collapse, and local lords eventually abandoned the haunted estate.

## What Actually Happened Here

Rather than a documented criminal case, Okiku and the Nine Plates represents a masterwork of Japanese cultural storytelling, evolving through centuries of theater, local folklore, and urban literature.

The earliest surviving text of the tale appeared in July 1741, when playwrights Asada Iccho and Tamenaga Tarobei I premiered a ningyo joruri puppet play titled Banshu Sarayashiki at the Toyotakeza theater in Osaka. This puppet production drew upon older regional oral traditions from Banshu, the historic province surrounding Himeji Castle in Hyogo Prefecture. Visitors to Himeji Castle today can still view Okiku-ido, a historic stone well on the castle grounds preserved as the traditional setting for the regional legend.

As the story moved east into the political capital of Edo, dramatists adapted the narrative for Kabuki theater, re-titling it Bancho Sarayashiki to reflect Bancho, an exclusive residential district in modern Tokyo where high-status samurai lived. The story underwent multiple revisions in 1824 and 1850 before playwright Okamoto Kido published his seminal 1916 modern play. Kido transformed the narrative from a gruesome horror tale into a poignant psychological tragedy, re-imagining Aoyama as a conflicted warrior testing Okiku's love rather than a simple villain.

The cultural power of the legend extended even into natural history. In 1795, a sudden outbreak of bagworm moth pupae affected trees near Himeji Castle. The chrysalises hung suspended by fine silk threads, resembling small bound human figures. Local residents promptly dubbed the insects Okiku-mushi, believing the spirit of the murdered maid had returned in nature.

In modern cinema, Okiku's lingering spirit established the visual motif for Asian psychological horror. The image of a pale maiden in white rising from a dark stone well served as the direct archetype for Sadako Yamamura in Koji Suzuki's iconic 1991 novel Ringu and its famous film adaptations, proving that Okiku's voice still resonates across Japanese storytelling.
