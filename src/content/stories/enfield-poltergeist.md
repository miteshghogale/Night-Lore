On a damp, humid evening on August 31, 1977, single mother Peggy Hodgson went upstairs to check on her four children inside their rented council house at 284 Green Street in Enfield, North London. Peggy shared the modest, semi-detached home with her daughters Margaret, aged thirteen, and Janet, aged eleven, alongside her sons Johnny, aged ten, and Billy, aged seven. Walking into the children's second-floor bedroom to tell them to stop bouncing on their mattresses, Peggy found Janet and Johnny sitting up in bed, pointing in terror toward the wall.

As Peggy stepped into the bedroom, a heavy oak chest of drawers began to slide across the carpet on its own, moving four feet toward the open doorway.

Thinking her children were playing a prank, Peggy grabbed the heavy furniture and pushed it back against the wall. The moment she released her grip, an unseen force pushed the chest back out into the room with equal strength, blocking the door. When Peggy tried to push it a second time, the heavy drawers slid open and closed rapidly, and a loud, rhythmic knocking sound resounded inside the plaster walls, shaking the floorboards beneath their feet.

Panicked, Peggy grabbed her children and ran next door to the home of her neighbors, Vic and Hazel Nottingham. Vic Nottingham, a burly builder, entered the Hodgson house carrying a heavy wooden cricket bat to search for intruders. As Nottingham walked through the ground-floor rooms, the loud knocking sounds followed him from room to room, echoing from the ceiling and walls. When Nottingham knocked three times on the wall with his knuckles, three distinct knocks answered back from the plaster. Unable to find any physical explanation, Nottingham called the Metropolitan Police.

Constables Carolyn Heeps and Richard Chislett arrived at the residence shortly before midnight. The officers inspected the perimeter, checked the gas and water meters, and searched the attic for intruders, finding nothing. As Woman Police Constable Carolyn Heeps stood inside the ground-floor living room talking to Peggy Hodgson, she watched a heavy upholstered armchair rise slightly off the carpet and slide four feet across the linoleum floor toward the window. Constable Heeps got down on her hands and knees, inspected the chair legs, checked the carpet for hidden wires, and examined the surrounding walls for magnets or levers. Finding no physical mechanism, Constable Heeps signed an official police affidavit recording that she had witnessed an unpowered piece of furniture move across a room without human contact.

Over the next eighteen months, investigators Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair from the Society for Psychical Research spent hundreds of hours inside 284 Green Street, documenting what became the most heavily monitored poltergeist case in British history.

The SPR investigators deployed tape recorders, infrared cameras, and environmental monitoring equipment across the house. Over eighteen months, Grosse and Playfair logged more than two thousand separate physical incidents. Small marbles and lego bricks flew across rooms, hitting investigators with heat radiating from their surfaces. Small fires ignited spontaneously inside closed kitchen drawers, house keys vanished from hooks and reappeared in locked attics, and heavy wool pillows were yanked out from beneath sleeping children. Photographer Graham Morris from the *Daily Mail*, using a motorized camera setup, captured famous sequences of photographs depicting eleven-year-old Janet floating horizontally in mid-air above her bed.

In December 1977, the case took an even stranger turn when eleven-year-old Janet began entering deep trance states, speaking in a harsh, guttural voice that emerged from her lower throat.

The voice identified itself as Bill Wilkins, declaring that he was a former resident who had lived and died in the house decades earlier. On audio recordings made by BBC Radio producer Rosalind Morris, the voice growled out specific details: "I became blind... then I had a hemorrhage and I fell asleep and I died in the chair in the corner downstairs." Medical specialists and speech pathologists who listened to the recordings noted that speaking using false vocal folds for hours at a time should have severely damaged a young girl's throat, yet medical examinations of Janet's larynx showed zero swelling or physical damage.

Following the BBC broadcast, Terry Wilkins, the surviving son of Bill Wilkins, contacted investigators. He confirmed that a man named Bill Wilkins had indeed lived at 284 Green Street years before the Hodgson family moved in, confirming that his father had become blind, suffered a fatal brain hemorrhage, and died in an armchair in the exact ground-floor corner described by the voice. Janet had no access to local land deeds or death certificates before the recordings were made.

## What Actually Happened Here

The Enfield case remains a landmark in psychical research because so many incidents occurred in bright light and were documented on audio tape, photograph, and television film.

Believers and anomalous researchers point to the sheer volume of neutral third-party testimony. Metropolitan Police officers, BBC sound technicians, independent engineers, and national newspaper journalists all signed statements confirming they saw heavy furniture move, heard disembodied voices, and experienced physical disruptions in well-lit rooms. Furthermore, the precise verification of Bill Wilkins' death by his surviving son provided strong evidence of anomalous information transfer that could not be explained by ordinary schoolgirl knowledge.

Skeptical researchers from psychical research organizations and the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry present a compelling counter-case built on juvenile trickery, exaggeration, and media circus dynamics.

Skeptics Anita Gregory and Ray Hyman set up hidden video cameras in an adjoining room and caught the children on tape bending metal spoons by hand when they believed no one was watching. In a 1980 television interview with ITV, Janet acknowledged that the children had staged isolated disturbances, stating: "Oh yeah, once or twice, just to see if Mr. Grosse and Mr. Playfair would catch us. They always did." Subsequent newspaper reports estimated that trickery accounted for roughly two percent of the overall phenomena.

Critiques of the photographic evidence demonstrate that Graham Morris' famous photographs of Janet floating above her mattress strongly resemble an athletic child jumping up and down on a spring mattress rather than genuine levitation. Furthermore, acoustic analysis performed by speech experts demonstrated that the guttural Bill Wilkins voice could be produced by anyone using false vocal fold vibration, a technique commonly used by throat singers and ventriloquists without causing permanent vocal cord injury.
