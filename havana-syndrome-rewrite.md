> **STATUS: UNRESOLVED**
> *Justification:* Despite dozens of official investigations by U.S. intelligence agencies, international scientific panels, and medical research centers since 2016, no single cause or perpetrator has been established, and evidence remains genuinely split between directed-energy and psychogenic hypotheses.

## The Sound Came From One Direction

It always started the same way. A sound, sharp and specific, seeming to come from one exact point in space. Screeching. Chirping. A piercing, metallic grating, as if something were drilling directly into the air beside your head.

Then the pressure. A tightness behind the eyes, a vibration deep in the skull, a feeling one diplomat compared to driving with the window cracked open at highway speed. It lasted anywhere from twenty seconds to half an hour, and then it stopped, leaving behind headaches, nausea, ringing ears, a mind that suddenly couldn't hold a thought.

The strangest part wasn't the sound. It was who didn't hear it. A spouse in the next room. A colleague two feet away in the same hotel corridor. Nothing. Whatever it was, it seemed to know exactly who to target and who to leave alone.

Beginning in late 2016, this happened to American and Canadian government personnel in Havana, Cuba, again and again, until it had happened to dozens of people, then hundreds, then, by some counts, more than a thousand, in over a dozen countries around the world. Eight years and at least a dozen separate official investigations later, nobody has been able to prove what it was.

## Havana, Late 2016

The earliest reports came quietly, out of quiet places: residential homes, hotel rooms, in the ordinary rhythm of diplomatic life in a country the United States had only recently reopened relations with. CIA officers. State Department staff. Family members. All of them describing the same strange sequence: the directional sound, the pressure, the sudden wave of illness that followed.

By June 2018, twenty-six Americans in Cuba had reported symptoms. The acute list read like a catalog of a concussion that never quite healed: severe dizziness, ear pain, tinnitus, hearing loss in a third of cases, blurred vision and light sensitivity in two out of three, cognitive fog that in some people never fully lifted. For many, what started as a single terrifying episode became a chronic condition, months of insomnia, balance problems, and a mind that no longer worked the way it used to.

The diplomatic fallout was immediate and severe. In August 2017, the U.S. State Department concluded the injuries were the result of either an attack or an unknown device, though it stopped short of formally blaming Havana. The U.S. expelled two Cuban diplomats that same month. Non-essential embassy staff were pulled out in September. In October, President Donald Trump called it a very unusual attack and named Cuba as responsible. Canada, watching its own diplomats fall ill, ended family postings to the country and eventually cut its embassy staff after a fourteenth Canadian reported symptoms in December 2018. Investigators on both sides of the border were left chasing a cause nobody could see, hear, or photograph.

## It Followed Them Around the World

If Havana syndrome had stayed in Havana, it might have remained a strange, contained mystery. It didn't.

In 2018, an American diplomat at the U.S. consulate in Guangzhou, China, began reporting the same symptoms, tracing them back to late 2017. By May, Secretary of State Mike Pompeo was testifying to Congress that what was happening in China was very similar to, and entirely consistent with, what had happened in Cuba. Diplomats were evacuated. A health warning went out across all of mainland China.

Then the map kept expanding. Moscow. Tbilisi. Poland. Taiwan. Australia, where the Russian embassy publicly denied any involvement. Serbia, where the CIA quietly evacuated an officer in 2021 over suspected exposure. London, where three White House staffers reported symptoms inside a single hotel in 2019. Hanoi, where two American diplomats were pulled out in August 2021, an incident serious enough to delay a scheduled visit from Vice President Kamala Harris. India, where an aide traveling with CIA Director William Burns came down with symptoms during an official visit that same September.

And then Vienna, which by 2021 had become the second Havana: dozens of American personnel and even children affected, a cluster so severe that the CIA recalled its own station chief over how slowly the response had moved. Even Washington wasn't safe. In 2019, a White House official walking her dog in a Virginia suburb was struck by the same symptoms. In November 2020, it happened again, on the Ellipse, the lawn just outside the White House itself. By early 2022, media estimates put the global total above a thousand reported cases. A 2024 government report confirmed 334 people had formally qualified for military health care over the injuries.

Nobody in the government, publicly, could say who was doing it, how, or why.

## The Case for a Weapon

The most direct explanation, the one that shaped years of foreign policy, was also the simplest: someone was aiming something at these people. A pulse of radio frequency or microwave energy, invisible, silent, capable of being fired at a single target through a wall or a window without leaving a mark.

In December 2020, a standing committee of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine looked at everything available, the symptom clusters, the directionality, the sudden onset, and concluded that directed, pulsed radio frequency energy was the single most plausible mechanism behind the acute cases. It wasn't proof. It was the closest thing to an educated guess that serious scientists were willing to put their names to.

Two years later, in February 2022, a scientific panel convened by the Director of National Intelligence went further, judging pulsed RF energy and ultrasound as plausible explanations specifically for the cases involving four particular "core" symptoms together. A separate report from the JASON Advisory Group, an elite panel of physicists that has advised the U.S. government for decades, released its own frequency analysis that same month. JASON ruled out ionizing radiation, acoustic energy, and radio frequencies below 500 megahertz or above 30 gigahertz entirely, while finding the narrow band between 500 megahertz and 30 gigahertz to be highly unlikely as a cause, without being able to rule it out completely.

A CIA interim report released that January had already reviewed roughly a thousand cases and found that most had ordinary explanations, malfunctioning air conditioning, undiagnosed illness, plain stress. But it also identified about two dozen cases where a foreign hand genuinely could not be ruled out. Two dozen, out of a thousand, that nobody could explain away.

## The Cricket in the Recording

While the weapon hypothesis moved through Washington, a very different kind of investigation was quietly unraveling the sound itself.

In 2018, the State Department handed the JASON Advisory Group actual audio recordings, captured by diplomats during real incidents in Havana. JASON's scientists studied them and concluded, with high confidence, that what they were hearing was most likely insects, not a weapon. No single energy source, they noted, could plausibly produce both the sounds on those recordings and the range of medical effects being reported.

Then, entirely separately, in January 2019, two biologists, Alexander L. Stubbs of UC Berkeley and Fernando Montealegre-Z of the University of Lincoln, ran their own independent analysis of the same recordings, matching the pulse rate, the frequency, the acoustic fingerprint. Working without any connection to JASON's classified process, they arrived at strikingly the same place: the calling song of the Indies short-tailed cricket.

Two separate teams, using two separate methods, both landed on an insect.

The medical evidence supporting an actual injury fared even worse under scrutiny. In 2018, University of Pennsylvania researchers examined twenty-one Havana diplomats and reported findings resembling brain injury, results that made headlines around the world. Then the same journal that published the study ran an editorial tearing it apart: no control group established before the fact, no baseline scans, heavy reliance on subjective self-reporting, MRI variations that could just as easily come from migraines or depression as from any attack. The neuroscience journal Cortex went further, publicly demanding the authors clarify their methods or retract the paper entirely, calling the flaws "gross." Neurologist Robert Baloh, reviewing the same data, didn't mince words. He called the conclusions science fiction.

In September 2021, the Cuban Academy of Sciences released its own independent report, prepared by twenty-one scientists, and reached a blunt verdict: no known form of energy could selectively injure the brain the way the reports described, and the most likely explanation was mass psychogenic illness, layered on top of pre-existing conditions nobody had bothered to check for beforehand.

## The Verdict, and the People Who Refused to Accept It

In March 2023, after more than two years of work, seven U.S. intelligence agencies released a joint assessment through the Office of the Director of National Intelligence. It remains, to date, the single most authoritative government judgment on Havana syndrome. Five agencies concluded it was very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible. One called it merely unlikely. One declined to take a position at all. CIA Director William Burns called it one of the most intensive investigations in the agency's history.

The reaction split almost exactly along the same lines the mystery itself had. John Bolton, formerly Trump's national security adviser, said he had never really believed Cuba had the will or the capability. Cuba's own vice foreign minister said the United States had weaponized the accusation itself, using it for years to justify isolating his country. And in the Senate, Marco Rubio flatly rejected the conclusion, insisting something real had happened to real people and that the investigation wasn't over.

A year later, in March 2024, the science caught up with the politics. Two major studies from the National Institutes of Health, tracking eighty-one and eighty-six affected federal employees over five years against carefully matched control groups, using the best neuroimaging and blood biomarker technology available, found no significant differences in brain structure, brain function, or any measurable biological marker. The dramatic 2018 findings could not be reproduced. What the studies did find was something quieter and sadder: many of the patients, whatever had first triggered their symptoms, were now living with real, serious levels of stress, anxiety, and depression that needed treatment in their own right.

## What's Still Unfolding

As recently as January 2025, the intelligence community reaffirmed its position: five of seven agencies still call foreign involvement very unlikely, even as two of them quietly shifted their internal thinking on whether an adversary might possess the technical capability at all, without concluding anyone had actually used it.

And the story hasn't stopped. In 2026, unverified reports surfaced that Homeland Security Investigations had covertly purchased a backpack-sized device, reportedly containing Russian-made components and capable of emitting pulsed radio frequencies, though officials and independent scientists alike remain openly skeptical it has anything to do with the actual cases. Separately, a Norwegian government scientist was reported to have built his own microwave-pulse device in 2024 and tested it on himself, producing symptoms that weren't an exact match, but that reignited the debate anyway. A new hypothesis, involving pulsed infrared lasers rather than microwaves, emerged from a Czech physicist in early 2026, and as of this writing, no one has found an obvious flaw in it, only unanswered questions about whether it could damage the retina. These are recent, unconfirmed threads, not settled findings, and should be read as exactly that: the mystery still writing itself. The year 2026 also marked the death of the first U.S. official who reported symptoms in Havana a decade earlier, a reminder that for those affected, the human consequences have outlived the answers.

Eight years after the first strange sound in Havana, nobody has produced the weapon. Nobody has proven it was never real. The evidence sits exactly where it started: split, unresolved, and still capable of ending a career, a posting, or in some cases, a person's health, without ever revealing what actually caused it.
